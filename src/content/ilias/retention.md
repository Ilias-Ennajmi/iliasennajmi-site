In 2018, a company called MoviePass had one of the best growth stories around. For $9.95 a month, you could see a film in a cinema every single day. People poured in. It went from around twenty thousand subscribers to more than three million in less than a year.

It was also losing money on almost every one of them. A single cinema ticket cost more than the monthly fee. The more people used the product, the faster the company bled. By September 2019 it had shut down.

I think about MoviePass whenever someone shows me a chart going up and to the right.

Growth is the most flattering number a company has. It's also one of the easiest to fake. Pour enough money into ads, discounts and free trials, and the chart goes up whether or not anyone sticks around.

Picture a bucket with a hole in the bottom. Turn the tap up high enough and the bucket looks full. It keeps looking full, right up until someone opens the water bill.

Retention is the unglamorous number underneath. How many of the people you won are still here in three months, or a year. Nobody puts it in the press release. Nobody rings a bell when a customer renews. But it's the number that decides whether a business is actually building something or just running on a treadmill.

There's a figure people in business love to quote, from work done at Bain & Company: a 5% increase in customer retention can lift profits by somewhere between 25% and 95%. People argue about the exact range, and it varies a lot by industry. The direction isn't really in dispute.

The reason is simple once you see it. Getting a new customer is expensive. You pay for the ad, the click, the discount, the welcome offer. A customer who stays pays that back, and then keeps paying. A customer who leaves after a month took the discount and went home, and you have to buy their replacement at the same price. Usually a bit more every year, because everyone else is bidding for them too.

So a business that keeps the people it wins gets to build on them. A business that keeps replacing them is running to stand still.

A lot of growth stories are really churn stories. They just got told before the churn showed up.

I understand why companies chase growth anyway. It's visible. It's exciting. It's what investors ask about first, and what teams get rewarded for. Nobody gets promoted for "the customers we already had mostly stayed." Retention work is slow and a bit boring. Fixing the welcome email. Answering support faster. Making the product slightly less annoying in forty small ways.

It also forces some uncomfortable questions. Why did they leave? Was the product ever as good as the ad said? Did we attract the wrong people with the wrong promise? Growth lets you put those questions off for a while. Retention makes you answer them.

Some of the best retention in the world right now belongs to a green owl. Duolingo built its product around the streak: the number of days in a row you've practiced. Miss a day and the streak resets. People keep streaks going for years. Some of them are genuinely learning a language. Some of them are mostly protecting a number, which is a kind of retention too, just a slightly haunted one.

There's a simpler test I like better. It comes from the growth marketer Sean Ellis, and it's one question. How would you feel if you could no longer use this product? If fewer than about 40% of your users say "very disappointed," you probably don't have something people really need yet. No amount of growth fixes that. It just helps you find out faster.

Acquisition is a loan. Retention is income. Mixing the two up is how confident companies go broke.

This isn't only true for businesses, by the way. Friendships you keep starting but don't keep up. The hobby that begins every January. A newsletter with ten thousand subscribers and nobody opening it. The number that feels good is rarely the number that matters.

The boring fix is to plug the hole before you turn up the tap. Earn the second visit. The renewal. The customer who comes back without an email nagging them to.

It's slower. It makes for terrible launch posts. It's also the only kind of growth that doesn't come with a countdown attached.

MoviePass, for what it's worth, came back a few years later under its founder, with a very different price. I hope they're watching the bucket this time.
