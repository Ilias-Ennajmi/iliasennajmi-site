There's a comforting idea in marketing that you can buy attention. Pay once and it's yours. Build an audience, bank it, keep it like a customer list.

You can't. You rent it. The lease is short, and the landlord raises the rent right after you've moved all your stuff in.

Every platform goes through the same cycle. At the start it's cheap, sometimes free, because it needs you more than you need it. Brands post, people see it, everybody's happy. Then the platform fills up and the algorithm tightens. The reach you used to get for nothing comes with a price tag, and the price keeps going up.

Facebook pages are the classic case. Plenty of brands built big followings there in the early 2010s, then watched their organic reach shrink to a small fraction of what it was, and got told the fix was to pay. Cory Doctorow has a word for this cycle, enshittification. Less polite than what we say in meetings. More accurate, too.

The platform was never your audience. It was the road between you and them, and whoever owns the road can charge whatever the traffic will bear.

> If one company can switch off your business by changing a setting, you were never the owner. You were a tenant.

I'm not saying stay off platforms. For most businesses that isn't realistic.

I'm saying use the rented reach to build something you actually own. An email list, or just a reason for people to come back that doesn't run through someone else's algorithm. Spend the rent money buying your way out of renting.
