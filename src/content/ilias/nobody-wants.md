There's an old marketing line: nobody wants a drill, they want a hole. Everyone in the industry has heard it. Most of us have said it in a meeting at least once, a bit too pleased with ourselves.

It doesn't go far enough. Nobody wants the hole either. They want the shelf. And not even the shelf, really. They want to be the kind of person who has shelves up, whose place looks sorted when friends come over. The drill is the last and least interesting link in a chain that starts with who someone is trying to become.

That's why feature lists sell worse than we'd like. A spec sheet answers a question the buyer isn't really asking. They're not comparing gigabytes. They're checking whether owning the thing makes them look a bit more like the person they picture when they picture themselves on a good day.

Look at any running shoe ad. Almost none of them talk about foam. They show someone at 6am, alone on an empty road, looking like they have their life together. That's what's for sale. The shoe is how you pay for it.

> Sell the person after, not the product before.

This can go too far, obviously. Promise an identity the product can't deliver and people notice, usually around the second time they use it. The features still have to be true.

But get the identity right and the features become proof. Get it wrong and there isn't a feature in the world that saves you.
