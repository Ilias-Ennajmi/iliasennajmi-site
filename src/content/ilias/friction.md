Signing up took thirty seconds and one tap. Cancelling takes a phone call, during business hours, in a timezone that isn't yours, with someone whose whole job is to talk you out of it.

That isn't bad design. That's the design working.

Friction is a lever, and most companies only pull it one way. Every step between you and paying gets sanded down. Every step between you and leaving gets a bit more gravel. In the short term the numbers look great. Churn goes down, someone gets a nice slide for the quarterly review.

But those customers aren't being retained. They're being held.

My favorite detail on this comes from the US Federal Trade Commission's lawsuit against Amazon over how hard it was to leave Prime. According to the complaint, the cancellation flow had an internal name: Iliad. As in the very long poem about a very long war. Somebody in that building has read Homer, and has a sense of humor about it.

> Every obstacle between a customer and the exit is a deposit in a resentment account, and it pays interest.

The bill shows up later, in the one channel nobody on the growth team controls: what people say about you when you're not in the room. The reviews. The screenshot of the cancel flow that does the rounds. The friend who asks if they should sign up and gets told "only if you enjoy phone calls."

Friction you add to trap people is borrowed goodwill. And the interest rate is brutal.
