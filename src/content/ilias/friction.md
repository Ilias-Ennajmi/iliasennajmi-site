Think about the last subscription you signed up for. How long did it take? Thirty seconds, maybe. Email, card, done. There was probably a free trial, and a big friendly button.

Now think about the last one you tried to cancel.

If you got lucky, it was a few clicks. If you didn't, you know how it goes. The cancel button lives four menus deep. Then there's a page asking if you're sure. Then an offer: 50% off if you stay. Then a survey, compulsory, about why you're leaving. And sometimes the final boss: to cancel, please call us, Monday to Friday, during business hours, in a timezone that isn't yours.

That isn't bad design. That's the design working.

In my industry we talk about friction all the time. Friction is anything that makes an action harder. An extra step, an extra field, an extra second of thinking. Remove friction and more people do the thing. Add it and fewer do. It's one of the most reliable levers there is.

Most companies pull it in only one direction. Every step between you and paying gets sanded down. Every step between you and leaving gets a bit more gravel.

A designer called Harry Brignull started collecting tricks like this in 2010 and gave them a name: dark patterns. This one he called the roach motel. Easy to get in. Hard to get out.

Gyms are the classic offline version. Joining takes five minutes and a smile at the front desk. Leaving, at plenty of them, has meant a letter, a notice period, a cancellation fee, or a trip back in person to a manager who's somehow never there. Everybody has a gym story. Nobody's gym story makes them want to join a gym.

Here's the part I find almost funny. In 1999, Amazon was granted a patent for 1-Click ordering. A button that let you buy something without even seeing a basket. It was one of the most celebrated pieces of friction removal in the history of online shopping, and the company guarded it for nearly twenty years.

The same company, it turns out, knew exactly how friction works in the other direction too.

It came out in 2023, when the US Federal Trade Commission sued Amazon over Prime. According to the complaint, cancelling meant getting through a four-page, six-click, fifteen-option process. And inside Amazon, that process had a name. Iliad.

As in the very long poem about a very long war. Somebody in that building had read Homer, and had a sense of humor about it. Amazon settled in 2025 for two and a half billion dollars, so the joke got expensive.

The Iliad, by the way, is called the Ilias in Greek. Which is also my name, and the name of half this website. I have mixed feelings.

Why does the maze work? Partly because every step is a chance to give up. Each screen costs a little patience, and at some point a lot of people decide the €12.99 isn't worth another phone call. And partly because each step shows you something you're about to lose. Your saved lists, your history, your streak. Losses feel bigger than gains, so the "are you sure?" screen does more work than most ads ever will.

In the short term, the numbers look great. Churn goes down. Someone gets a very nice slide in the quarterly review.

But those customers aren't being retained. They're being held.

And held customers behave differently from happy ones. They don't recommend you. They don't forgive you when something goes wrong. They leave the moment it gets easy, and they tell people why. Every obstacle you put between a customer and the exit is a deposit in a resentment account, and it pays interest.

The bill shows up later, in the one channel nobody on the growth team controls: what people say about you when you're not in the room. The reviews. The screenshot of the cancel flow that does the rounds. The friend who asks if they should sign up and gets told "only if you enjoy phone calls."

Regulators have started to catch up. Germany now requires a cancel button on websites that sell subscriptions. Other places are heading the same way. But I don't think the law is really the point. The point is that friction you add to trap someone is borrowed goodwill, and the interest rate is brutal.

The companies I actually trust, the ones I'd recommend without thinking about it, have one thing in common. Leaving is as easy as joining. Which is exactly why I don't leave.

If a business needs a maze to keep you, the maze is telling you something about the business.
