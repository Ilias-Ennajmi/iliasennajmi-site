When Steve Jobs came back to Apple in 1997, the company was months away from running out of money. It was also selling a remarkable number of things. Dozens of computer models, printers, a digital camera, a handheld gadget called the Newton. Slightly different versions for slightly different shops, with names nobody could keep straight.

In one of his early meetings, the story goes, Jobs asked a simple question. Which of these should I tell my friends to buy? Nobody could give him a straight answer.

So he drew a grid on a whiteboard. Two columns: consumer and professional. Two rows: desktop and portable. Four boxes. Apple would make one great product for each box, and nothing else. Most of the product line was cut.

Later that year, on stage, he explained the thinking. People assume focus means saying yes to the thing you're focusing on, he said. It actually means saying no to the hundred other good ideas.

Apple isn't a small, scrappy company anymore, and I'm not writing a fan letter. But that grid is still the clearest picture of strategy I know.

Because most documents called "strategy" aren't strategy. I've read plenty of them. They're lists of ambitions. Every market, every audience, every channel, every opportunity, all at the same time, with a nice cover slide and the word "holistic" somewhere on page three.

Nothing in them costs anything. Every option stays open, every audience is a target, and nobody had to lose an argument to write it. That's the tell. A real strategy always makes somebody in the room unhappy.

Southwest Airlines has a story about this that gets told a lot. For years, the company had one clear idea: be the low-fare airline. The legend goes that when someone brought research to the chief executive, Herb Kelleher, showing passengers would like a light chicken salad on longer flights, he asked one question. Will adding a chicken salad make us the low-fare airline? No? Then no chicken salad.

It sounds almost petty. It's actually the whole point. A clear enough strategy makes a thousand small decisions for you, including the ones that sound nice.

Michael Porter, probably the most quoted strategy professor there is, put it more formally in 1996. The essence of strategy, he wrote, is choosing what not to do. It's one of the most repeated lines in business schools and one of the most ignored in boardrooms.

Why is it so hard? Because saying no feels like losing money you haven't made yet. Every customer you decide not to serve is revenue you're walking away from, at least on paper. Every feature you refuse to build is a request from someone important you'll have to disappoint. Saying yes to everything feels safe. It even feels like ambition.

But a brand that tries not to put anyone off usually ends up not pulling anyone in. If you want to be the obvious answer for someone, you have to be the wrong answer for somebody else, and be fine with it. The hotel that doesn't take kids becomes the favorite of every couple who wanted a quiet weekend. The restaurant with four things on the menu gets famous for those four things. The shop that doesn't try to sell everything becomes the place you go for the one thing.

In marketing we say that if you're talking to everyone, you're talking to no one. It sounds like a poster. It also happens to be true.

It works on a personal level too, which is the part I find harder. Every yes to one thing is a no to something else, whether you say it out loud or not. The extra project is a no to your evenings. The new commitment is a no to the old one you already didn't have time for. People who seem focused aren't doing more. They've just made their noes on purpose, instead of letting them happen by accident.

Here's a simple test, for a company or a person. Name the customer you're happy to lose. The feature you'll never build. The opportunity you'd turn down even if it came with money attached.

If you can't name any of them, you don't have a strategy yet. You have a hope, and probably a slide deck.

And if you're reading this with twelve priorities for the quarter, I'm sorry. Pick four. Draw the grid.
