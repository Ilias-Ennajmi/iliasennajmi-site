Every time you remember something, you're not playing back a recording. You're rebuilding it. And you rebuild it for whoever is doing the remembering today, which isn't quite the person it happened to.

This isn't a figure of speech. Elizabeth Loftus spent decades showing how easy memory is to bend. In one of her early experiments, people watched a film of a car crash and were then asked how fast the cars were going when they "hit" each other, or when they "smashed" into each other. A week later, the "smashed" group was more likely to remember broken glass. There wasn't any.

One word, a week, and a brand new detail. So the past isn't a place you visit. It's a draft.

I don't think that's lying, really. It's closer to maintenance. A decision that looked reckless at the time becomes brave in memory, or inevitable, or somebody else's fault. The alternative is carrying it around as a plain mistake, and that's heavy. So we edit. We give ourselves a version of the past we can afford to live next to.

> We don't remember what happened. We remember what we needed it to mean.

Knowing this won't make your memory any more accurate. What it can do is make you a bit gentler with other people's versions of events, since they're doing the same thing you are.

And a bit more suspicious of your own. Especially the stories where you come out looking good. Those are the ones that got the most editing.
