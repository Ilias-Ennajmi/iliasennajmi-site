Think of a big decision you made years ago that turned out well. Moving somewhere. Leaving a job. Ending something, or starting something.

Now try to remember how you felt about it at the time. Not how you tell the story now. How it actually felt, that week.

Most people can't. And the ones who think they can are probably wrong.

Every time you remember something, you're not playing back a recording. You're rebuilding it, from whatever pieces are lying around. And you rebuild it for whoever is doing the remembering today, which isn't quite the person it happened to.

This isn't a figure of speech. It's one of the sturdiest findings in psychology.

In 1974, Elizabeth Loftus showed people a short film of a car crash. Afterwards, she asked some of them how fast the cars were going when they "hit" each other. She asked the others how fast they were going when they "smashed" into each other. The "smashed" group guessed higher speeds. And a week later, they were more than twice as likely to remember seeing broken glass.

There was no broken glass in the film.

One word, one week, and a detail that never existed. Loftus spent the rest of her career showing how far this goes. In later studies, people came to remember being lost in a shopping mall as a child, sometimes with vivid detail, when it never happened. Memory isn't a hard drive. It's more like a story told at every family dinner, a little different each time, until nobody knows which version came first.

So the past isn't a place you visit. It's a draft.

What's interesting is which way the edits tend to go. They're not random. They tend to go in our favor.

Psychologists have a name for part of it: choice-supportive bias. After we choose something, we remember the option we picked as better than it was, and the ones we turned down as worse. The job you left gets a little more toxic every year. The apartment you didn't take develops problems it never had. The decision that looked reckless at the time becomes brave, or inevitable, or somebody else's fault.

I don't think that's lying, really. It's closer to maintenance. Carrying every choice around as a plain, unedited mistake would be exhausting. So we edit. We give ourselves the version of the past we can afford to live next to.

Brands rely on a version of this too. Once you've bought something, you want to have made a good choice. So you notice the good reviews, skip the bad ones, and tell your friends it's great. Buyer's remorse is real, but its opposite is more common. The product doesn't need to be perfect. It just needs to give you a story you can defend.

Holidays are a good example of the edit in action. Researchers have followed people before, during and after trips, and found a pattern often called rosy retrospection. Beforehand, people are excited. During the trip, they're more mixed: the delays, the heat, the argument at dinner. Afterwards, the memory gets better again, often better than the trip actually felt while it was happening. The good bits stay. The waiting in queues fades.

Daniel Kahneman had a name for the part of you doing this: the remembering self, as opposed to the experiencing self who was actually there. The remembering self doesn't keep a full record. It mostly keeps the most intense moment and the ending. In one well-known medical study, patients remembered a longer, more uncomfortable procedure as less unpleasant, simply because its last few minutes were gentler.

Your phone has made this stronger, not weaker. The photo keeps the one moment everyone was smiling. Nobody photographs the queue.

There's a cost to all this, of course. If you're always editing the past to protect the present, you don't learn much from it. The same mistake can happen three times and feel different each time, because each time you told yourself a different story about why.

There's another cost that's easier to miss. Everyone around you is editing too. Your version of the argument, your ex's version, your sibling's version of your childhood. They're not necessarily lying either. They're doing maintenance, same as you.

Knowing this won't make your memory any more accurate. I've tried. What it can do is make you a bit gentler with other people's versions of events, since they're doing exactly what you're doing.

And a bit more suspicious of your own. Especially the stories where you come out looking good.

Those are usually the ones that got the most editing.
