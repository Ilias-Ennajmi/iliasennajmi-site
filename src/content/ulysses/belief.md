Everyone has that one friend. Smart, well read, genuinely good at their job. And there's one topic where, the moment it comes up, they turn into someone you don't recognize.

Maybe it's a diet. Maybe it's crypto, or a politician, or a theory about what's really in the water. You bring up a fact and they have three counter-facts ready, with sources. You walk away with a slight headache and the feeling that you somehow lost an argument you were right about.

If you're honest, you're probably also that friend to somebody. I'm fairly sure I am.

We like to think bad beliefs come from not thinking hard enough. Give people more information, more education, more intelligence, and the bad ideas fall away. It's a lovely theory. It's also, as far as the research can tell, mostly wrong.

Here's the uncomfortable version. Intelligence makes you a better lawyer, not a better judge.

A judge weighs the evidence and then decides. A lawyer already knows which side they're on and goes looking for evidence that helps. Most of us, most of the time, think like lawyers and feel like judges. Psychologists call this motivated reasoning. The name makes it sound like a rare malfunction. It's closer to the factory setting.

And being smart just gives the lawyer a bigger budget.

There's a study I think about a lot. In 2013, a team led by Dan Kahan at Yale gave people a small table of numbers and asked what it showed. For some people, the table was about whether a skin cream worked. For others, the exact same numbers were about whether banning concealed guns reduced crime.

On skin cream, the people who were best with numbers got it right most often. No surprise there. On guns, something strange happened. The people who were best with numbers got it right when the answer matched their politics, and got it wrong when it didn't. Their skill didn't protect them. It gave them more ways to talk themselves out of what the table said.

They didn't get dumber for that one question. They pointed the same brain at a different job.

This is why arguing with facts so rarely works. You're not arguing with what the person knows. You're arguing with what the belief does for them. A lot of our beliefs aren't just opinions. They're part of who we are and who we belong with. Changing your mind can feel less like updating a spreadsheet and more like leaving a group.

The most extreme version of this I know comes from the 1950s. A psychologist called Leon Festinger heard about a small group near Chicago whose leader said she had received messages from another planet. The world, the messages said, would be destroyed by a great flood before dawn on December 21st, and the believers would be rescued by a flying saucer. Some of them quit their jobs. Some gave away their money and their possessions.

Festinger and two colleagues joined the group, undercover, to see what would happen when the flood didn't come.

It didn't come. And the most committed members didn't give up. After a long, awful night, a new message arrived: their faith had saved the world. The group, which had been fairly private until then, started calling newspapers and trying to recruit. When reality disagreed with the belief, a lot of them chose the belief, and doubled down.

That's an extreme case. But the move itself is ordinary. The more you've invested in a belief, the more it costs to drop it, and the more creative your inner lawyer gets.

It's also, not by coincidence, how a lot of marketing works. Nobody in my industry tries to win you over with a table of numbers. We give you a side to be on. Once you're on it, you'll do the arguing for us, and you'll do it better than any ad could, because you'll mean it. If you don't believe me, try telling someone their phone is worse than yours.

The internet made all of this easier. You can now find a well-produced video supporting almost any position, made by someone with a nice microphone and a confident face. If you already want to believe something, the evidence is one search away. The lawyer never runs out of material.

I don't have a clean fix, and I'm suspicious of anyone who says they do. Reading more won't do it on its own. You'll just read the things that agree with you, faster.

The best I've got is structural. Go find people who disagree with you and aren't stupid. That second part matters. It's easy to feel smart next to the worst version of the other side, and the internet is always happy to serve you the worst version of the other side. Find the best one. Read it like you're trying to understand it, not beat it. Notice how uncomfortable that is.

And once in a while, pick something you believe strongly and ask yourself what it would take to change your mind. If the honest answer is "nothing," that's worth knowing too.

If every smart person you know already agrees with you, that's not proof you're right. It might just mean you've stopped looking.
