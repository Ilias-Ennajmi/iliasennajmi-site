At 9am, I'm a reasonable person. I eat properly, I answer emails calmly, and I definitely don't need to check my phone every four minutes.

At 11pm, I'm someone else. That guy orders food. That guy watches one more episode. That guy has opinions in comment sections.

Same person. Same values. Same goals. Different time of day.

We talk about willpower as if it were character. You either have discipline or you don't, and if you don't, that says something about who you are. It's one of the most common ways people judge themselves, and each other.

I think it works more like a bank account. You wake up with a balance. Every small no costs a little. No to the pastry in the office. No to the angry reply. No to the thing in the ad. By the evening, after a day of small refusals, the account is close to empty. And that's when the most tempting offers tend to show up.

Now I have to be honest, because this is where the science gets messy.

In the late nineties, a psychologist called Roy Baumeister ran a famous experiment. People sat in a room that smelled of freshly baked cookies. Some were allowed to eat the cookies. Others had to eat radishes instead and leave the cookies alone. Then everyone got a puzzle that couldn't be solved. The radish group gave up much sooner. Resisting the cookies seemed to have used something up.

It became known as ego depletion, and it turned into one of the most popular ideas in psychology. Self-control as a muscle that gets tired. Hundreds of studies followed.

Then, in 2016, more than twenty labs around the world tried to repeat the core effect together, with the same method, at scale. They found almost nothing. The debate since has been loud and unresolved. Maybe the effect is smaller than anyone thought. Maybe it depends on what you believe about willpower in the first place. Maybe it was never really there.

So I'm not going to pretend there's a tank in your head that runs dry at 6pm.

But you don't need a lab to notice that tired you makes different decisions than rested you. Everybody knows it. And more to the point, the people selling to you know it.

The supermarket doesn't put sweets at the checkout by accident. It's the one spot where you're tired, bored, stuck in a queue and holding a basket you've already said no to twenty times. The streaming service doesn't start the next episode in five seconds because it's convenient. It's because in five seconds you won't get around to deciding. Delivery apps know exactly what time of night their orders spike.

Nobody's testing your virtue at 11pm. They're checking your balance.

Here's the part I find genuinely useful. The people who look incredibly disciplined usually aren't winning more fights than you. They're having fewer of them.

There's research on this too, and it's held up better. People who score high on self-control don't seem to spend their days heroically resisting temptation. They report running into fewer temptations in the first place. They've set up their lives so the fight rarely comes up.

Even the most famous willpower experiment of all points the same way. In the marshmallow test, from the late sixties onwards, Walter Mischel's team left young children alone with one marshmallow and a deal: eat it now, or wait fifteen minutes and get two. Later studies suggested the kids who waited did better in life, and the test became a symbol of self-control as a kind of inner strength. (A large follow-up in 2018 found that link much weaker than first reported, once family background was taken into account.)

But look at what the kids who waited were actually doing. They weren't staring at the marshmallow and resisting. They covered their eyes. They turned around. They sang to themselves, kicked the table, played with their hair. The ones who lasted were the ones who found a way not to be in the fight at all.

The snacks never make it into the house. The phone charges in another room. The running shoes live by the door. The savings leave the account automatically on payday, before there's a chance to spend them. None of that takes willpower in the moment. It took one decision, once, on a good morning.

That's the whole trick, really. Make your decisions at 9am, for the version of you that shows up at 11pm.

You don't need more willpower. You need fewer moments where you have to use it.

Which, if you think about it, is exactly what the supermarket figured out first. They just set it up for themselves.
